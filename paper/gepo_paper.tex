\begin{abstract}
We present a six-year backtest of a weekly credit-spread strategy on the
SP100 universe in which a Greek-filtered candidate pool is ranked each week
by the intrinsic GROUND ratio in its \emph{Kelly-EV-with-entropic-discount}
form:
\[
\GROUND(p, x, k) \;=\; \bigl(\exp(g(p, x)) - 1\bigr) \cdot \exp\!\bigl(-k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| u)\bigr),
\]
where $g(p, x) = E_p[\ln(1 + w^\star X)]$ is the per-trade Kelly
log-growth at the optimal sizing fraction $w^\star$, so $\exp(g) - 1$ is
the \emph{Kelly EV} --- the variance-adjusted expected wealth gain per
trade at Kelly sizing --- and $\exp(-k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}})$ is an
entropic ambiguity discount in $(0, 1]$. The score is dimensionless and
reads directly as \emph{percent risk-adjusted expected return per
trade}. We restrict the menu to growth-positive candidates ($g > 0$).
For small $g$, $\exp(g) - 1 \approx g$, so $\ln \GROUND \approx g - k
\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}} = J_k$, the multiplier-preferences functional of
\citet{hansen2008robustness} and \citet{maccheroni2006ambiguity}: the
canonical Kelly-EV ranker is the small-quantity, economically-readable
form of the Hansen-Sargent ranker, and the two rank near-equivalently in
the canonical credit-spread regime. The intrinsic GROUND ratio is the v2
of the original GROUND construction of \citet{mercurio2020}, replacing
the comparative form against a per-week reference candidate with a
per-candidate intrinsic score. The amplification parameter $k = 20$ is
selected from an in-sample sweep on the 2020--2024 window before any
2025--2026 data is scored. Kelly payoffs are the per-spread $\{+b,
+\alpha b, -1\}$ for $b = \text{credit}/\text{max-loss}$ with the
principled partial multiplier $\alpha(b) = (b-1)/(2b)$. We require both
legs to carry at least 100 contracts of open interest. For backtest
reporting we apply a conservative $b \le 5$ cap so the headline
numbers do not depend on the $\sim 1$--$2\%$ of trades with $b > 5$ ---
production accepts any $b$ through the OI gate. On the in-sample window the
strategy posts Sharpe $1.63$, Calmar $4.03$, and a bankroll-independent
wager yield of $15.4\%$ at zero slippage; on the strict 2025--2026
holdout (350 trades, 70 weeks, never used in $k$-selection) it posts
Sharpe $2.99$, Calmar $12.44$, and yield $31.0\%$. SPY buy-and-hold over the same windows posts Sharpe
0.79--1.11. Single-factor baselines that rank by Kelly-growth $g$ alone
or KL-divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ alone are reported for comparison.
The data give direct empirical evidence that GROUND's joint growth-and-
divergence penalty produces a useful trade-selection signal in a real
options market with realistic execution costs.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

The GROUND ratio was introduced in \citet{mercurio2020} as a portfolio
ranking criterion that simultaneously rewards Kelly growth and penalizes
deviation from a uniform reference distribution. In this paper we adopt
the intrinsic v2 form in its \emph{Kelly-EV-with-entropic-discount}
canonical (Section~\ref{sec:ground-v2}):
\begin{equation*}
\GROUND(p, x, k) \;=\; \bigl(\exp(g(p, x)) - 1\bigr) \cdot \exp\!\bigl(-k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| u)\bigr),
\end{equation*}
where $g(p, x) = E_p[\ln(1 + w^\star X)]$ is the per-trade Kelly
log-growth at the optimal sizing fraction $w^\star$ on the per-spread
payoffs $\{+b, +\alpha b, -1\}$. The first factor $\exp(g) - 1$ is the
\emph{Kelly EV}: the variance-adjusted expected wealth gain per trade at
Kelly sizing (log-utility expectation bakes in variance via Jensen). The
second factor $\exp(-k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}})$ is the entropic ambiguity
discount in $(0, 1]$. Their product is dimensionless and reads as
percent risk-adjusted expected return per trade.

\paragraph{Connection to Hansen-Sargent.}
Taylor-expanding the first factor, $\exp(g) - 1 \approx g$ for small
$g$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
\ln \GROUND(p, x, k) \;=\; \ln\!\bigl(\exp(g) - 1\bigr) - k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\;\approx\; g - k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\;=\; J_k(p, x)
\end{equation*}
in the canonical regime where $g$ is small (per-trade log-growth at
Kelly sizing typically sits in $[0.001, 0.10]$ nats on this universe).
$J_k$ is the multiplier-preferences functional of
\citet{hansen2008robustness} and the variational preferences class of
\citet{maccheroni2006ambiguity}. The canonical Kelly-EV ranker is thus
the \emph{economically-readable small-quantity approximation} of the
Hansen-Sargent ranker, gaining direct interpretation as a per-trade
risk-adjusted return while preserving the theoretical lineage. Concretely,
for a candidate trade with three discrete outcomes (full-win, partial,
full-loss) under empirical probabilities $(p, r_o, q)$ and an alpha
parameter $\alpha$ giving the partial-loss multiple, GROUND combines
\begin{itemize}
    \item the Kelly-optimal growth rate $G(p, q, r_o; \alpha)$, the
        per-trade wealth multiplier computed at the closed-form
        $w^\star$ solving the standard Kelly-with-partials first-order
        condition;
    \item the Kullback--Leibler divergence
        $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{p} \| \mathbf{u})$ from the uniform
        three-outcome distribution.
\end{itemize}
The intuition is straightforward: $G$ measures the trade's
\emph{expected return} (the Kelly-optimal per-trade growth), while
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ measures the trade's \emph{risk}. The use of
information-theoretic quantities as risk measures in portfolio
optimization was first proposed in \citet{mercurio2020portfolio},
where we argued that entropy provides a natural alternative to
variance for problems in which the return distribution is discrete or
otherwise ill-suited to mean-variance treatment. We subsequently
specialized the construction to binary options
\citep{mercurio2020binary}, showing that $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ of the
wager's outcome distribution against a uniform reference is a
\emph{convex risk measure} in the sense of \citet{follmer2002},
and that the uniform distribution itself plays the role of the
minimum-risk wager. The present paper builds directly on those two
foundations, extending the construction from the two-outcome
(binary-option) setting to the three-outcome
(vertical-credit-spread) setting and embedding it inside the GROUND
return-over-risk ratio. Conventional risk measures --- variance,
value-at-risk, conditional VaR --- are defined on continuous return
distributions and become degenerate or ill-conditioned on a small
finite outcome space, which is exactly the gap
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ fills: it is bounded in $[0, 1]$ (in base-$n$ units
on an $n$-state outcome space), it is monotone in the concentration
of the implied distribution, and it inherits its convex risk-measure
properties from the binary-option case under the natural extension to
$n$ states.

The strength of GROUND comes from holding these two quantities ---
return ($G$) and risk ($D_{\mathrm{KL}}$) --- in a single ratio. A
trade with high $G$ but very high $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is a sharp bet on
a near-certain outcome whose growth comes from informational
concentration that may not survive estimation noise; such a trade is
discounted by the denominator. A trade with high $G$ and modest
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is an information-light, growth-dense bet that the
ratio rewards. The empirical work that follows will show that this
return-over-risk reading of the ratio is what does the work in
practice.

The original paper presented GROUND on synthetic three-outcome problems
and a closed-form Kelly setting. The work reported here is, to our
knowledge, the first end-to-end backtest of GROUND-driven trade selection
on a real options market, including realistic candidate generation,
filtering, and execution costs. We also adopt a simplified
\emph{intrinsic} form of GROUND that drops the per-week
minimum-divergence reference $R_b$ originally used to normalise the
denominator (Section~\ref{sec:ground-v2}). The intrinsic form is
algebraically simpler, computationally trivial, and empirically
indistinguishable from the reference-normalised form across our sweep.

The intrinsic GROUND ratio in natural-log units, valid for any
(finite or continuous) outcome space, is
$\Gamma_k(p, x) = G(p, x) / \exp(k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| u))$.
The present paper applies this to a three-outcome credit-spread
outcome space — the three states being full-win (underlying expires
outside the short strike), partial (underlying expires between the
two strikes), and full-loss (underlying expires beyond the long
strike) — with the uniform reference $u$ over those three states.
Both $G$ and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ are computed in nats (natural log);
the only $n = 3$
appearance is the dimension of the outcome distribution
$(p, r_o, q)$.

The paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} describes
the data, candidate generation, filters, sizing, and slippage treatment.
Section~\ref{sec:ground-v2} states the GROUND v2 formula precisely and
notes our log-base canon. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the in-sample
2020--2024 window, the strict 2025--2026 holdout, the full extended
2020--2026 sample, an SPY buy-and-hold benchmark, the $k$-sweep table, and
single-factor baselines that strip GROUND down to its $G$ and
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ components. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
limitations.


\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

This paper sits at the intersection of four literatures: the Kelly criterion
applied to derivatives, information-theoretic portfolio selection, systematic
short-volatility option strategies, and ranking-based trade selection. We
review each briefly to position the GROUND ratio's contribution.

\subsection{Kelly criterion in derivatives}

The Kelly criterion \citep{kelly1956} prescribes the bet-sizing fraction that
maximises expected log-wealth, and is the growth-optimal strategy in repeated
play. Its application to financial markets traces to \citet{thorp2006} and is
developed comprehensively in \citet{maclean2011}, including extensions to
fractional-Kelly sizing under estimation error and to multi-outcome bets such
as those characteristic of options spreads. \citet{vince1990} extended Kelly
to bounded-loss instruments where the gambler-style $f^\star$ does not apply
directly; credit spreads, with their bounded per-spread maximum loss, fall
naturally into this regime.

The GROUND framework extends this lineage in two ways. First, the closed-form
$w^\star$ used in the Kelly-growth term $G$ solves the first-order condition
for a three-outcome bet (full-win, partial, full-loss), which matches the
realised P\&L distribution of a held-to-expiry credit spread more faithfully
than a binary win/loss formulation. Second, single-factor ranking by $G$
alone is empirically unstable when probability estimates are noisy; the
GROUND ratio addresses this by penalising the divergence of the candidate's
outcome distribution from uniform, effectively discounting candidates whose
growth advantage relies on a sharp probability concentration. We report
$G$-only and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$-only baselines in
Section~\ref{sec:baselines} as direct evidence.

\subsection{Information-theoretic portfolio selection}

The use of information-theoretic quantities in portfolio choice begins with
\citet{cover1991universal}, whose universal portfolios attain the growth rate
of the best constant-rebalanced portfolio in hindsight without distributional
assumptions, and \citet{algoet1988asymptotic}, who established the asymptotic
optimality of the log-optimal portfolio under general stationary processes.
\citet{cover2006} provide the canonical reference for the
Kullback--Leibler divergence and its role in characterising the cost of
distributional misspecification. The GROUND ratio's $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ term
sits in this tradition: it measures the informational distance between the
candidate's empirical outcome distribution and a maximally-uncertain uniform
reference, and uses that distance as a regulariser on the
growth-maximisation objective.

The pair-trading antecedent \citep{mercurio2020} that introduced the GROUND
formulation used the divergence term in a comparative
(candidate-against-reference) form. The intrinsic v2 form adopted in the
present work removes the cross-candidate coupling and exposes
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ as a per-candidate regulariser, which is the more natural
formulation for streaming weekly selection.

\subsection{Systematic short-volatility strategies}

The realised behaviour of the strategy described here\,---\,collecting weekly
credit on bounded-loss vertical spreads, gated by a trend filter\,---\,is
adjacent to the systematic short-volatility literature. The CBOE PUT and
WPUT indices \citep{cboe_put} provide the canonical benchmark for short
cash-secured put strategies; \citet{israelovnielsen2014} document the
volatility risk premium that makes these strategies profitable in
expectation, and \citet{israelov2017} characterise their drawdown profile in
adverse regimes. Vertical credit spreads differ from short cash-secured puts
in that maximum loss is bounded per contract, which materially changes the
drawdown profile but preserves the underlying short-vol payoff structure.

Where the present work departs from this literature is in the
\emph{selection} step. Index strategies (PUT, WPUT) are mechanical:
short-the-ATM-put on a fixed schedule. The GROUND-driven strategy is
selective: at each weekly entry it ranks a Greek-filtered candidate pool of
up to several hundred spreads and chooses the top five. The empirical
question this paper addresses is whether the ranking step adds value over
mechanical or single-factor alternatives; Section~\ref{sec:results}
(in particular the single-factor baselines) provides the affirmative answer.

\subsection{Ranking-based trade selection}

Ranking-based selection is widely used in equity factor strategies
\citep{fama1992,asness2013} but is less well developed in derivatives.
\citet{goyenko2022} document that option mispricing signals can be ranked to
construct outperforming portfolios on the equity-options cross-section.
\citet{cao2023} apply machine-learning ranking to option returns directly.
The GROUND ratio differs from these in that it is a closed-form,
parameter-light ranker (one tuneable amplification factor $k$) derived from
first principles rather than fit to data; the only data-tuned parameter is
$k$, which we sweep on the in-sample window and freeze before the holdout
(Section~\ref{sec:k-sweep}).


\section{Strategy and Methodology}\label{sec:strategy}

\subsection{Universe and instrument}

The traded universe is the SP100 constituent set as of 2020-01-01, held
fixed throughout the backtest. We do not reconstruct point-in-time
membership; this introduces survivorship bias, since names that joined
the index after 2020-01-01 are excluded throughout, and names that
were dropped from the index during the window remain in the candidate
pool for their full backtest lifetime. The bias direction is ambiguous
but non-zero, and we make no attempt to quantify it.

At each weekly entry date we consider \emph{both} a vertical bull-put
spread and a vertical bear-call spread on each underlying that has a
Greek-feasible candidate pair, and let GROUND select between them.

Concretely, for each underlying and entry date the candidate generator
emits all $(K_{\text{short}}, K_{\text{long}})$ pairs satisfying six
filters: (i) days-to-expiry between three and eight, capturing the
near-weekly expiration cycle; (ii) short-leg $|\Delta|$ in
$[0.35, 0.65]$ with the target at $|\Delta| = 0.50$, so the short
strike is roughly at-the-money; (iii) a per-share max-loss cap of \$5,
which controls dollar risk per spread; (iv) a positive net credit, to
exclude net-debit fills; (v) a credit-to-max-loss ratio of at least
$0.30$, meaning the spread must collect at least 30~cents per dollar
at risk, which filters out the under-priced wide-strike spreads whose
credits are implausibly small relative to their width; and (vi) a
\emph{both-leg} open-interest minimum of $100$ contracts, a liquidity
gate that screens out strikes whose quotes may be stale or
non-tradable. The both-leg requirement is material: an earlier
configuration that applied the gate only to the short leg admitted
spreads whose long leg was at extremely low OI, and these were
disproportionately spreads whose displayed mid implied a credit
collecting the majority of the spread width --- artifacts of stale
bid-ask quotes rather than tradable signal.

\paragraph{Candidate pool size.}
Each weekly entry produces a candidate pool that exceeds the top-$N=5$
cap in every week of the backtest --- 1{,}286 trades over 260 in-sample
weeks is essentially $5\times$ the week count (fourteen weeks have one
fewer pick because a candidate's Kelly quadratic admits no in-$(0,1)$
root and is dropped), indicating the cap binds essentially 100\%
of the time. Across the in-sample window 77 distinct underlyings were
filled at least once. We do not log per-week pool sizes, but the
candidate pool is large enough every week that GROUND is doing real
selection, not picking from a pool of one or two.

\subsection{Macro filter}

A single macroeconomic filter is applied: SPY's close must be above its
trailing 100-day SMA for bull-put fills, and below for bear-call fills.
This is a binary regime gate, not a continuous tilt. Per-ticker regime
gating, gap filters, low-VIX filters, and theta/credit ratio filters
were all evaluated and found to be either redundant or
return-degrading; only the SPY regime gate is kept in the canonical
configuration.

\subsection{GROUND v2: intrinsic form}\label{sec:ground-v2}

The original GROUND ratio of \citet{mercurio2020} is a
\emph{comparative} score: a candidate $a$ is ranked against a
per-week reference candidate $b$ (typically the minimum-divergence
member of the pool) by the ratio of their log-growth difference to
their KL-divergence difference:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{a \mid b} \;=\; \frac{g_a - g_b}{D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(a)} - D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(b)}},
    \qquad g := \ln G.
    \label{eq:ground-paper}
\end{equation}
This comparative form introduces a cross-candidate coupling (the
selection of $a$ depends on the reference $b$) that is awkward in a
streaming weekly-selection setting.

This paper introduces the \emph{intrinsic} GROUND ratio, a
per-candidate score that requires no reference, in its canonical
\emph{Kelly-EV-with-entropic-discount} form:
\begin{equation}
    \GROUND(a, k) \;=\; \bigl(\exp(g_a) - 1\bigr) \cdot \exp\!\bigl(-k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(a)}\bigr),
    \qquad g_a > 0,\ k = 20.
    \label{eq:ground-canonical}
\end{equation}
The first factor $\exp(g_a) - 1$ is the \emph{Kelly EV} of candidate
$a$: the expected wealth gain per trade at Kelly-optimal sizing.
Because $g_a = E_{p_a}[\ln(1 + w^\star X)]$ is the expectation of a
concave (log) function, Jensen's inequality bakes a variance penalty
into $g_a$ for free, so $\exp(g_a) - 1$ is variance-adjusted by
construction --- a high-EV high-variance trade has lower $g$ than a
moderate-EV low-variance trade with the same arithmetic EV. The
second factor $\exp(-k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}})$ is the entropic
ambiguity discount, taking values in $(0, 1]$, smaller when the
implied distribution is more concentrated relative to uniform. The
product is a dimensionless fractional return reading directly as
\emph{percent risk-adjusted expected return per trade}: under the
canonical $k = 20$ and natural-log units, a typical selected trade has
$\GROUND \in [0.5\%, 8\%]$, with top picks in the high single digits.
We restrict the menu to $g_a > 0$ (growth-positive candidates only);
$g \le 0$ candidates have no risk-adjusted return and are dropped
before ranking.

\paragraph{Small-$g$ connection to Hansen-Sargent multiplier preferences.}
Taylor-expanding the first factor, $\exp(g) - 1 = g + g^2/2 + O(g^3)$.
In the canonical credit-spread regime $g \in [0.001, 0.10]$ nats, so
$\exp(g) - 1 \approx g$ to within 5\% across the entire selected
range. Taking the log of \eqref{eq:ground-canonical},
\begin{equation}
    \ln \GROUND(a, k) \;=\; \ln\!\bigl(\exp(g_a) - 1\bigr) - k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(a)}
    \;\approx\; g_a - k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(a)}
    \;=\; J_k(a),
    \label{eq:ground-hs-link}
\end{equation}
where $J_k = g - k \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is the multiplier-preferences
functional of \citet{hansen2008robustness} and the variational
preferences class of \citet{maccheroni2006ambiguity}. The canonical
Kelly-EV ranker is thus the small-$g$ economically-readable form of
the Hansen-Sargent ranker: it preserves the theoretical lineage but
trades the analytically-clean additive form for direct interpretation
as a per-trade return. We adopt the Kelly-EV canonical for
display and selection consistency.

The amplification factor $k$ controls how aggressively concentration
risk is discounted; we selected $k$ by sweeping $\{0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10,
20\}$ on the 2020--2024 in-sample window. The full sweep is reported
in Table~\ref{tab:k-sweep}; $k = 20$ is the in-sample Sharpe optimum.
We froze it before scoring any 2025+ data.

\paragraph{Per-spread Kelly payoffs.}
We use the actual credit-spread payoff geometry rather than the
binary-option normalization $b=1$, $\alpha = -1/2$. For a spread
collecting net credit $c$ on max-loss $m$, the win pays $b = c/m$ on
each unit at risk and the full-loss pays $-1$. The partial zone
$[K_{\text{long}}, K_{\text{short}}]$ has linear payoff in the
underlying terminal price, ranging from $+b$ at $K_{\text{short}}$ to
$-1$ at $K_{\text{long}}$. Assuming uniform expiry within the partial
zone the mean per-unit P\&L is $(b-1)/2$; equating this to $\alpha b$
gives the per-spread principled partial multiplier
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(b) \;=\; \frac{b - 1}{2b}.
    \label{eq:alpha-b}
\end{equation}
For the binary case $b = 1$ this reduces to $\alpha = 0$ (partial $=$
wash). For typical credit spreads $b \in (0.3, 0.8)$, $\alpha(b)$
ranges from $-1.17$ to $-0.13$: partials are heavily punitive for
small-credit, wide-strike spreads and only mildly so for tight,
high-credit spreads. The Kelly first-order condition with outcomes
$\{+b, +\alpha b, -1\}$ is a quadratic in $w$ which we solve in
closed form; details in Appendix~A.

\paragraph{Logarithm base.} A credit spread has three discrete
outcomes: full-win, partial, and full-loss. We compute $G$ and
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ in natural-log units (nats), the canonical base for
Kelly log-utility and for information-theoretic divergence. The
uniform-outcome entropy is $H(\mathbf{u}) = \ln 3 \approx 1.099$ and
$D_{\mathrm{KL}} \in [0, \ln 3]$. Any other log base produces an
algebraically equivalent ranker at a re-scaled $k$, so the choice of
base is purely a unit convention; we adopt natural log throughout for
its state-space agnostic generality (the same formulas apply
unchanged to binary, 3-state, or continuous-outcome settings).

\subsection{Sizing}

Two flat-sizing regimes are reported, $q \in \{1, 2\}$ contracts per
trade, with starting bankroll \$10{,}000. Under flat sizing the GROUND
ranking is bankroll-independent: the top-$N$ ordering at each weekly
entry depends only on per-spread economics, not on the running bankroll.
Consequently the $q=1$ and $q=2$ trade lists are identical; their
bankroll trajectories differ exactly by a factor of two (modulo the
floor at $\$0.01$ which is never hit in our runs).

\subsection{Slippage treatment}

We treat slippage as a \emph{post-hoc} P\&L haircut, not a
trade-selection input. Concretely, we run the backtest once at zero
slippage, recording the canonical trade list, and then apply a per-leg
haircut $s \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ cents per leg to each fill. The haircut enters both the realised P\&L and the
``wagered'' figure used in the yield calculation (since slippage
inflates the effective max-loss per share).

This separation is important. If slippage is fed into trade selection,
the resulting trade list is a \emph{different} strategy at each
slippage level, and aggregate-statistics comparisons across slippage
become apples-to-oranges. With post-hoc treatment, we report a single
trade list under four execution-cost regimes.

\subsection{Bankroll-independent yield}

Conventional total-ROI is bankroll-dependent: a strategy that
turns \$10k into \$30k can be reported as ``200\% ROI'' but those
percentages do not translate to a different starting bankroll because
flat sizing does not compound. We instead report a sportsbook-style
\emph{yield}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{yield} \;=\; \frac{\sum_t q_t \cdot \text{pnl}_t \cdot 100}
                          {\sum_t q_t \cdot (\text{max\_loss}_t + 2s) \cdot 100} \;\times\; 100\%,
\label{eq:yield}
\end{equation}
where $q_t$ is the number of contracts entered in trade $t$,
$\text{pnl}_t$ is the realised per-share profit or loss (already net
of slippage), $\text{max\_loss}_t$ is the mid-mid maximum loss
per share, $s$ is the per-leg slippage in dollars, and the factor of
100 converts per-share dollars into per-contract dollars (each option
contract covers 100 shares). The $+2s$ term in the denominator is the
slippage-inclusive maximum loss: a credit spread has two legs (a
short and a long), and slippage of $s$ per leg means the trader
actually risks an extra $2s$ per share beyond the mid-mid max-loss.
The yield so defined is the ratio of dollars earned to dollars
actually put at risk, invariant to starting bankroll and dependent
only on per-trade economics. We retain conventional final-bankroll,
Sharpe, and max drawdown statistics for cross-comparability with
other backtests.


\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We report three windows. The \emph{in-sample} 2020--2024 window is the
window on which $k=20$ was selected from a sweep over $\{0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}$.
The \emph{holdout} 2025--2026 window contains 350 trades over 70 weeks
that were never used in $k$ selection or any other tuning; this is the
clean out-of-sample test. The \emph{extended sample} 2020--2026 window
combines the two; it inflates trade count and is reported only to make
clear what changes when 2025--2026 is appended to the train window
(and to anchor against the multi-slippage equity curves shown in the
weekly HTML reports). The headline scientific claim rests on the
holdout, not the extended sample.

All numbers are at the canonical configuration described in
Section~\ref{sec:strategy}: ranking by Kelly EV $\cdot \exp(-k \cdot
D_{\mathrm{KL}})$ at $k = 20$, $g > 0$ filter, top-$N=5$ per week,
natural-log canon. Reported yields are gross of commissions; a
commission-inclusive row is added in
Section~\ref{sec:results-commissions}. We also report Calmar (annualised
return $/$ max drawdown), which proved a more discriminating risk-
adjustment metric than Sharpe for this strategy --- the canonical
Kelly-EV ranker concentrates its wins (raising weekly variance and
shaving Sharpe slightly) while keeping drawdowns equal-or-smaller,
which Calmar correctly rewards.

\subsection{Holdout vs.\ in-sample}

On the strict 2025--2026 holdout, Sharpe is 2.99 and yield is 31.0\%
at zero slippage. Both are higher than the in-sample window, not lower.
We do not over-interpret this: the holdout is short (70 weeks) and
2025--2026 was a generally favourable regime for the underlying SPY
trend filter (SPY closed above its 100-day SMA most of the holdout).
The fact that the strategy does not \emph{collapse} on unseen data is
the load-bearing claim; the apparent improvement is consistent with
sampling noise plus regime tailwind, both well within the Mertens
95\% CI on the holdout Sharpe (Table~\ref{tab:sharpe-ci}).

\paragraph{April 2025 correction.}
The holdout includes the late-March / April 2025 tariff-shock
correction in equity markets. Across the nine-week window 2025-03-17
through 2025-05-12, the strategy posted net P\&L $+\$1{,}084$ at $q=1$
with a within-window Sharpe of $1.86$ --- below the full-holdout
$2.99$ but still positive and consistent with the in-sample
distribution. The single largest week of the entire holdout
(\$1{,}104 on 2025-04-14) and the single worst week (\$-529 on
2025-03-17) both fall inside this window: the strategy absorbed both
the down-leg of the correction and the rebound without regime-flip
intervention.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to extreme weeks.}
Holdout Sharpe excluding the best and worst five weekly returns
(60 weeks remaining) is $4.06$; excluding the best and worst ten
weeks (50 weeks) is $5.10$. Both are higher than the headline
$2.99$, indicating that the result does not depend on a small number
of outlier weeks. The trimmed distribution shows the typical week
is more consistent than the full-sample standard deviation
suggests --- the best and worst weeks roughly balance.

\subsubsection{Confidence intervals on Sharpe}\label{sec:sharpe-ci}

Sharpe ratio estimates from short samples have wide confidence intervals,
and we report them here to forestall over-reading of the headline
numbers. \citet{lo2002} derives the asymptotic distribution of the
estimated Sharpe ratio under the assumption of i.i.d.\ Gaussian returns:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_w \overset{a}{\sim}
\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathrm{SR}_w,\;
\frac{1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{SR}_w^2}{T}\right),
\label{eq:lo-sharpe}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the number of return observations. There is, however, a
problem in applying this directly to a credit-spread strategy: weekly
P\&L is mechanically bounded above by the total credit collected and
below by the total max-loss, which gives the return distribution a
sharp left skew and heavy tails. The Gaussian assumption underlying
\eqref{eq:lo-sharpe} is therefore violated in the very direction that
matters most for risk reporting.

\citet{mertens2002} (also reproduced in \citet{opdyke2007})
provides a higher-moment-adjusted variance that remains valid under
i.i.d.\ but non-Gaussian returns:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_w)
  = \frac{1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{SR}_w^2 \;-\; \gamma_3 \mathrm{SR}_w
            \;+\; \tfrac{1}{4}\gamma_4 \mathrm{SR}_w^2}{T}.
\label{eq:mertens-sharpe}
\end{equation}
Here $\gamma_3$ is the sample skewness of the weekly returns and
$\gamma_4$ is the sample excess kurtosis. Under the canonical
per-spread Kelly payoffs $\{+b, +\alpha(b) b, -1\}$ and the $k = 20$
ambiguity-aversion setting, the strategy's weekly return distribution
exhibits modest positive skewness on the holdout
($\gamma_3 = +0.45$) and roughly symmetric distributions on the longer
windows ($\gamma_3 \in [-0.03, +0.12]$). Excess kurtosis is positive
but mild ($\gamma_4 \in [+0.22, +1.22]$). Mechanically, positive
$\gamma_3$ \emph{narrows} the Mertens variance relative to Lo (the
$-\gamma_3 \mathrm{SR}$ term reduces the numerator), while positive
$\gamma_4$ widens it; the two effects partly cancel. In our setting
Mertens intervals are within a few percent of Lo on every strategy
window. We report both for transparency.

Annualised standard errors are obtained by scaling
\eqref{eq:mertens-sharpe} by $\sqrt{52}$, and 95\% intervals by
$\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{ann}} \pm 1.96 \cdot
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{ann}})$. Both Lo and Mertens
intervals are reported in Table~\ref{tab:sharpe-ci}.

weekly observations is still a wide interval. The in-sample interval
$[0.86,\,2.62]$ is tighter and exceeds SPY's matched-window interval
$[-0.12,\,1.69]$, but since $k$ was selected on this window the
in-sample Sharpe is not a clean estimate of the strategy's true
Sharpe.

Second, the extended-sample row is the most defensible single number

has the largest $T$, the tightest confidence interval, and combines

windows are not independent, so this comparison is descriptive rather
than a formal test of difference.

Weekly aggregation removes within-week selection correlation but does
not eliminate autocorrelation across weeks under regime shifts. We do
not adjust for serial dependence here; the practical implication is
that all reported intervals should still be read as lower bounds on
uncertainty.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why GROUND vs.\ simpler rankers}

We compare GROUND against two single-factor baselines (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}):
$G$-only, which ranks by Kelly-growth and ignores $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$,
and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$-only, which ranks by lowest divergence and ignores
$G$. The full GROUND ranker outperforms both on Sharpe and yield on the
in-sample window (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). The two components are
individually weak signals; their multiplicative combination via
Equation~\eqref{eq:ground-canonical} is where the empirical edge appears.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Survivorship bias.} The SP100 constituent list is held
        fixed at the 2020-01-01 composition. Names that joined the
        index after that date are excluded, and names that left the
        index during the window remain in the candidate pool. The
        bias direction is ambiguous and unquantified.
    \item \emph{Holdout length.} The strict 2025--2026 holdout is
        70 weeks, which is enough to test for collapse but not enough
        to characterise the distribution of returns. Sharpe estimates
        from a 70-week sample have wide confidence intervals.
    \item \emph{Greek-only candidate generator.} We do not blend
        implied volatility with realised volatility or use
        drift-adjusted probabilities in the canonical configuration.
        Both were tested as toggles in earlier iterations and found to
        be non-improving on Sharpe under the GROUND ranker.
    \item \emph{Fill assumption.} Mid-mid quotes are taken as
        executable, with the post-hoc per-leg haircut serving as the
        execution-cost regime.
    \item \emph{Commissions.} Section~\ref{sec:results-commissions}
        reports a commission-inclusive estimate; for a retail account
        at $\sim\$0.65$/contract per leg this shifts yields by roughly
        one slippage column.
    \item \emph{Single macro filter.} The SPY-100d-SMA gate is binary
        and global. A more sophisticated regime model would likely
        improve performance but would also increase overfitting risk
        and was deliberately excluded from the canonical configuration.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusion}

Under the canonical form $\GROUND = (\exp(g) - 1) \cdot \exp(-k D_{\mathrm{KL}})$
with $k = 20$, per-spread Kelly payoffs $\{+b, +\alpha(b) b, -1\}$,
a both-leg open-interest gate at 100 contracts, and a
credit-to-max-loss cap at $b \le 5$ (backtest reporting convention; production has no cap), the GROUND ranker yields
Sharpe $1.63$ and Calmar $4.03$ on the 2020--2024 in-sample window,
and Sharpe $2.99$, Calmar $12.44$ on the 350-trade, 70-week strict
2025--2026 holdout, against SPY buy-and-hold Sharpes of 0.79 and 1.11
over the same windows. Under a 3\textcent/leg post-hoc execution
haircut, yield falls from $15.4\%$ to $6.6\%$ in-sample and from
$31.0\%$ to $21.0\%$ on the holdout. Single-factor baselines that
strip out either $g$ or $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ underperform the combined
ranker (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\section{Closed-form Kelly-growth score with a partial outcome}
\label{app:kelly-foc}

Section~\ref{sec:strategy} references the Kelly-growth score $G$ used as
the numerator of the GROUND ratio, computed at a closed-form
$w^\star$. We derive that fraction here.

\paragraph{Score, not sizing.}
The Kelly-growth $G$ used in this work is a \emph{ranking score}, not a
prescription for how many contracts to trade. The actual sizing rule is
flat ($q=1$ or $q=2$ contracts per spread, capped at
\texttt{MAX\_CONTRACTS}); $w^\star$ never enters portfolio construction.
The canonical scoring uses the actual per-spread three-outcome
payoffs $\{+b, +\alpha(b) b, -1\}$ where $b = c/m$ is the
credit-to-max-loss ratio and $\alpha(b) = (b-1)/(2b)$ is the
principled partial multiplier (Section~\ref{sec:ground-v2}).
We first develop the closed-form $w^\star$ for the normalised case
$b = 1$, $\alpha = -1/2$ (which recovers \citet{mercurio2020} eq.~28
and corresponds to a binary credit spread); the general per-spread
case follows by the same algebra and is given in the closing
paragraph.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Consider a wager with three outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item full win, with probability $p$ and per-dollar payoff $+1$;
  \item partial outcome, with probability $r_o$ and per-dollar payoff
        $+\alpha$, where $\alpha \in [-1, 1]$ is the partial multiplier;
  \item full loss, with probability $q = 1 - p - r_o$ and per-dollar
        payoff $-1$.
\end{itemize}
The constant $\alpha = -0.5$ used in \citet{mercurio2020} eq.~28
corresponds to the binary $b=1$ case with the uniform-in-strikes
partial average $(b-1)/2 = 0 = \alpha \cdot b$ at $\alpha = -1/2$;
this is exactly the limit of the principled $\alpha(b) = (b-1)/(2b)$
as $b \to 1$ from below. We use it as the normalised reference
geometry in this appendix and replace $\alpha$ with the per-spread
$\alpha(b)$ and $\pm 1$ payoffs with $\{+b, -1\}$ in the closing
paragraph. The probability $q$ is decomposed from the short-leg
ITM probability via $q = \Delta_{\text{short}} \cdot
\texttt{LOSS\_FACTOR}$ with $\texttt{LOSS\_FACTOR} = 0.60$ (canonical),
splitting the ITM mass between full-loss and partial outcomes.

\paragraph{Growth function.}
If a fraction $w \in [0, 1]$ of the (notional) bankroll is wagered, the
bankroll multiplier in each outcome is $1 + w$, $1 + w\alpha$, and
$1 - w$ respectively. The Kelly growth rate $G$ is the geometric mean
of these multipliers under the probabilities $(p, r_o, q)$, equivalently
the exponential of the expected log-multiplier:
\begin{equation}
\ln G(w; p, r_o, q, \alpha)
  = p \log(1 + w) + r_o \log(1 + w\alpha) + q \log(1 - w),
\qquad
G = \exp\bigl(\ln G\bigr).
\label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}
The Kelly criterion maximises $\ln G$ (equivalently $G$) over $w$.

Differentiating with respect to $w$ and setting the derivative to zero
gives the first-order condition
\begin{equation}
\frac{p}{1 + w}
+ \frac{r_o\, \alpha}{1 + w \alpha}
- \frac{q}{1 - w} = 0.
\label{eq:foc}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Closed form.}
Multiplying both sides of \eqref{eq:foc} by $(1+w)(1+w\alpha)(1-w)$ and
collecting powers of $w$ yields the quadratic
\begin{equation}
A w^2 + B w + C = 0,
\label{eq:quadratic}
\end{equation}
with coefficients
\begin{align*}
A &= -\alpha\,(p + r_o + q) = -\alpha, \\
B &= \alpha (p - q) - (p + q), \\
C &= p + r_o\, \alpha - q.
\end{align*}
The first equality in $A$ uses $p + r_o + q = 1$. The growth-optimal
fraction $w^\star$ is the unique root of \eqref{eq:quadratic} in
$(0, 1)$:
\begin{equation}
w^\star = \frac{-B - \sqrt{B^2 - 4AC}}{2A}.
\label{eq:wstar}
\end{equation}
For the canonical $\alpha = -0.5$, $A > 0$ and the in-unit root is
uniquely $w^\star$ above. The clearing of the $(1+w\alpha)$ factor
introduces a spurious second root outside $(0, 1)$ which is discarded.
The Kelly growth rate $G$ used in the GROUND ratio is then
\begin{equation}
\ln G = p \ln(1 + w^\star) + r_o \ln(1 + w^\star \alpha)
      + q \ln(1 - w^\star),
\qquad
G = \exp(\ln G),
\label{eq:G-used}
\end{equation}
the per-trade wealth multiplier evaluated at the Kelly-optimal sizing
fraction. The internal computation is in log space (nats), where
$\ln G$ and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ share units, and exponentiated to
obtain the growth rate $G$ used in
\eqref{eq:ground-canonical}.

\paragraph{Boundary cases.}
When $\alpha = -1$ the partial outcome is indistinguishable from a full
loss; combining $r_o$ into $q$ recovers the standard symmetric binary
Kelly $w^\star = p - (r_o + q) = 2p - 1$. When $\alpha = 1$ the partial
outcome is indistinguishable from a full win; the analogous combination
with $p$ gives $w^\star = (p + r_o) - q = 1 - 2q$. When $\alpha = 0$ the
partial outcome is a wash and \eqref{eq:foc} reduces to a binary Kelly
on the renormalised pair $(p, q)$ with $w^\star = (p - q) / (p + q)$.

\paragraph{Per-spread generalisation: $\{+b, +\alpha b, -1\}$.}
The canonical ranker uses the actual per-spread payoffs rather than the
normalised $\{+1, +\alpha, -1\}$. Replacing the win payoff $+1$ with $+b$
and the partial payoff $+\alpha$ with $+\alpha b$, the log-growth
becomes
\begin{equation}
\ln G(w; p, r_o, q, b, \alpha)
  = p \log(1 + w b) + r_o \log(1 + w\alpha b) + q \log(1 - w).
\label{eq:growth-b}
\end{equation}
The FOC \eqref{eq:foc} generalises to the same quadratic
$A w^2 + B w + C = 0$ with
\begin{align*}
A &= -\alpha b^2, \\
B &= \alpha b^2 (p + r_o) - b\bigl[p + r_o\,\alpha + q(1 + \alpha)\bigr], \\
C &= p b + r_o\, \alpha b - q.
\end{align*}
We solve the quadratic in closed form and pick the unique root in
$(0, 1)$ as $w^\star$. The partial multiplier is taken to be
$\alpha(b) = (b-1)/(2b)$, the uniform-in-strikes mean of the linear
partial payoff between $K_{\text{long}}$ and $K_{\text{short}}$
(Section~\ref{sec:ground-v2}). At $b = 1$ this collapses to the
binary case above; at smaller $b$ the partial is heavily penalised,
which is appropriate for thin-credit, wide-strike spreads where the
partial zone is large relative to the credit. The Kelly growth rate $G$
used in the GROUND ratio is $\exp(\ln G)$ with \eqref{eq:growth-b}
evaluated at $w^\star$.

\paragraph{Empirical probability estimation.}
The probabilities $(p, r_o, q)$ are estimated from a Black--Scholes
risk-neutral distribution under the canonical configuration, with
short-leg implied volatility used as the volatility input and
$\texttt{LOSS\_FACTOR}$ splitting the ITM mass. We tested
realised-volatility, drift-adjusted, and blended IV/RV estimators in
earlier iterations and found no Sharpe improvement under the GROUND
ranker; this robustness to the probability-estimation choice is
consistent with the intuition that GROUND's $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ regulariser
absorbs much of the estimator's distributional error.
